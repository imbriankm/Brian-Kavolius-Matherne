## The challenge

It is no secret that arts professionals have careers that are difficult to get, difficult to keep, and pay below what we might expect for the level of training that they have often received. For example, an orchestra player in The United States receives a national average salary of about $45k per year. (https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Salaries/What-Is-the-Average-Orchestra-Musician-Salary-by-State)

Entrepreneurship skills, although not the answer to every artistic career woe, have not been very well taught at the University level. Unemployed artistic graduates are in need of these career skills, and they often find that they are difficult to get. The market has risen to address this problem, with the creation of a multitude of career coaching programs. These programs are often aimed at this exact type of client: Recently graduated from a university arts program, and frustrated with the limited options available to them out of school.

This creates an imbalanced relationship. Desperate professionals who have achieved career competency in their craft that are entering a market that doesn't value those skills, meeting established professionals (often looking for side work), who can promise what the graduates most want, "a career in the arts".

I suspect that the program pricing can reach a height that is much higher than the value of what is actually being provided. Since the graduates need is existential, in identity if not in practice, these programs can charge a price that far exceeds the on ground value that graduates end up receiving. Although the skills are developed, students are often paying too high a price for one more spin of the roulette reel.

This paper aims to understand pricing models of these programs when compared to the product itself. What are the customers receiving, and how much are they paying for it? The customer can, before they sign up for the program, know the price of the program, the length of it, some things about the coach, and they may even have some client testimonial. However, they cannot know the future impact on their career, the quality of the content, or the veracity of the claims of the marketing. Finally, because of the desperation of their position, they are more likely to inappropriately value the marketing claims.

Part of this research will be to determine if coaches do use refunds, outcome based fees, or published results. It's likely that some do. ## Desperation pricing or ordinary price discrimination?

_Professor Stauffer's Q1 asks what the price data would have to show to tell desperation pricing apart from pricing on what's delivered and from ordinary price discrimination. My answer so far covers what's delivered (coach contact time) but not price discrimination._

I don't think these programs use price discrimination. A student rush ticket or an early bird price is typically for smaller products, like a $40 ticket or a $8 drink special. These are very expensive programs.

It is possible that people negotiate with these coaches about the sticker price. However, the answer that matters is the very start of the process. What is the sticker price before modifications? My hypothesis is that the sticker price for these programs is inflated at the start.

_Note (Claude's wording, from my argument above): my argument isn't that these programs never discount. Early-bird prices, payment plans and negotiation may all happen, but they start from the sticker price, and the sticker price is what I think is inflated._

I think "applying" is a marketing tactic to induce the feeling of scarcity. They are targeting high achievers just out of school. Applying for things is in their DNA.

If coaching programs have inflated beyond $150 per contact hour, then we can think they're overpriced. $150 comes from university lessons. My understanding of industry standards is that a lesson with a great player (music), is between $85 and $130. The research will work if we find 30 programs at least.
